            A Season in an Afternoon
            
  
            Role Call: A Collective Teachers' Self-Portrait
            
  
            A field guide to making a podcast with the people in the room and the phones in their pockets: method, questions, recording, care, sound, publishing and tools. Built for Art Day at FIU, where Miami-Dade teachers make Role Call, a season in two hours.

            The idea
            
  
            A podcast does not need a host, a studio or a budget. It needs a sequence of good questions and people willing to answer them for each other.
            
  
            The method here is simple: everyone answers the same questions, in pairs, on their own phones. The recordings then take one of two forms.
            
  
              
    
            1. The season. One question is one episode. Each episode is a collage of many voices answering one thing, and thirteen questions make a season.
              2. 
    
            2. The collage. Instead of individual episodes, all the questions are blended into a single sound collage, designed for an exhibition space or a single listener. It flows organically or dynamically by theme, and can even loop endlessly.
              3. 
  
            
  
            Either way, a room of strangers makes a portrait of itself.
            
  
            It works for a school, a studio, a congregation, a block, a family, a team. Anywhere people share a connection but rarely hear each other describe it.
            

            

            
  
            The method
            
  
            How the Art Day session runs, in two hours. The result is Role Call: A Collective Teachers' Self-Portrait, a podcast season made by the people in it.
            
  
              
    
            1. Open with your own answer. The facilitator tells a short, true story that answers the first questions out loud. Three to four minutes. It shows the depth you are asking for before you ask for it. At Art Day 2026, for Dimitry Saïd Chamy, it is a grandmother who trained as a dressmaker, became a teacher, and whose teaching made a family's life possible.
              2. 
    
            2. Pair up with someone you don't know. Strangers listen better than colleagues; they can't skip ahead.
              3. 
    
            3. Spread out. Corners, hallways, outside. Distance between pairs is the cheapest noise control there is. Some room tone is fine; it says where this happened.
              4. 
    
            4. Record a consent slate first. Question 0: the partner repeats a short consent line after the interviewer, with their name and a spoken yes to taking part.
              5. 
    
            5. One reads, one answers, then swap. The interviewer reads each question aloud and records the answer, sound only, in the voice memo app on the partner's phone (see Recording with what you have). Answers go to one shared place (at Art Day, a MASV upload portal where each teacher sends all their recordings at once; see Uploading your recordings); sort them by question afterward.
              6. 
    
            6. Hour two: pass the mic. The whole room comes back together to say what surprised them, what they recognized, what stayed. Record this too. It is often the best episode.
              7. 
  
            

            

            
  
            The questions
            
  
            The Art Day sequence, written for teachers. Borrow the arc, not the words: rewrite each one for your own people.
            
  
              
    
            1. The slate. Repeat after me: "My name is ___, and I consent to being recorded for Role Call: A Collective Teachers' Self-Portrait, and to this recording being published."
              2. 
    
            2. How long have you been a teacher, and what inspired you to enter the field?
              3. 
    
            3. Think back to the first person who recognized your creativity and your gifts. How did they encourage you, and what example did they set that you carry with you today?
              4. 
    
            4. Was there a teacher in your life, inside or outside the classroom, who opened a door for you, changed something fundamental? What did they do or say?
              5. 
    
            5. Have you ever thanked that person? If you did, what did you say? If you didn't, what would you want to say to them now?
              6. 
    
            6. What's something on your desk that has nothing to do with teaching, but reminds you why you're here?
              7. 
    
            7. Is there something you wear or carry every day when you enter the classroom? What does it do for you?
              8. 
    
            8. What was it like being a student at the age your students are now, and what's different today?
              9. 
    
            9. Describe how one student made you realize how important your role was as a teacher.
              10. 
    
            10. How does technology empower you as a teacher, what does it do for your students as learners, and where does it become a problem you have to mitigate?
              11. 
    
            11. What's a lesson that flopped spectacularly, and what did you learn from it?
              12. 
    
            12. What do you wish the public understood about this job?
              13. 
    
            13. If you could design one day in the school year from scratch, what would it look like?
              14. 
    
            14. What would you tell teachers in the future about the times we are living in now?
              15. 
    
            15. How has this experience made you feel? And is there any feedback you'd like to give the organizers?
              16. 
  
            
  
            The arc: who you are, who made you, what you keep close, then outward into the work, its failures and its public, then a message to the future. The last question is for the organizers rather than an episode: how the session felt, and anything they should hear, whether a testimonial or a critique.
            

            

            
  
            Writing your own questions
            
  
              
    
            1. Start easy. The first question should be answerable by anyone in under a minute. It warms the voice and the nerves.
              2. 
    
            2. Go back before you go forward. Lineage questions (who saw you first, who opened a door) earn trust and produce stories, not opinions.
              3. 
    
            3. Ask about objects. A desk thing, a ring, a jacket. Things are concrete; people talk about them freely and reveal a lot. Ask them to describe it: the listener can't see it.
              4. 
    
            4. Save the hard questions for the middle. By then people answer differently than they would have at the start.
              5. 
    
            5. Give permission to be honest. A three-part question that ends on the problem ("and where does it go wrong?") gets past the brochure answer.
              6. 
    
            6. Don't build in assumptions. Check every question for facts it takes for granted: that people grew up here, went to these schools, have children, have a partner. One wrong premise stalls the answer.
              7. 
    
            7. Ask one thing at a time, or one thing in clearly ordered parts. Read it aloud before you use it; if you stumble, rewrite it.
              8. 
    
            8. End facing forward. A message to the future closes a season better than a summary.
              9. 
  
            

            

            
  
            Recording with what you have
            
  
            Sound only: this is an audio podcast, not a video one. Everyone records with the voice memo app already on their phone. A phone held well beats an expensive microphone used badly. The room and the distance matter more than the gear.
            
  
              
    
            1. Find soft surroundings. Carpet, curtains, couches, coats, trees. Avoid glass, tile and empty halls; they echo.
              2. 
    
            2. Hold the phone a hand's width from the mouth, microphone end pointed at the speaker, and keep it there. Distance that changes is louder and quieter by turns.
              3. 
    
            3. Hold it still. Fingers sliding on the case are loud. Rest elbows on a table or knee.
              4. 
    
            4. Outside, shelter from wind. Turn your back to it, or stand by a wall. A foam cover or even a sock over the microphone helps.
              5. 
    
            5. Turn on Do Not Disturb or airplane mode, unless the recording needs the network to upload.
              6. 
    
            6. Record ten seconds and play it back before the first real answer. Every time.
              7. 
    
            7. Keep a backup. If the answer matters, record on a second phone too.
              8. 
    
            8. Let silences run. Wait three seconds after an answer seems over. The best line often comes next.
              9. 
  
            
  
            On an iPhone: Voice Memos
            
  
              
    
            1. Before the session, set the quality: Settings > Apps > Voice Memos > Audio Quality > Lossless. (On older iOS versions, Settings > Voice Memos.) Lossless files are larger; if a phone is short on space, Lossy is fine.
              2. 
    
            2. Record: open Voice Memos and tap the red Record button. Tap stop when the answer is over; the memo saves itself.
              3. 
    
            3. Name it straight away: tap the recording's name and type the question number and the speaker, like Q03 Maria. The slate is Q00. Named files sort themselves.
              4. 
    
            4. To upload: tap the recording, then More (⋯) > Share > Save to Files. Then open the upload link in Safari and add the recordings from Files. Voice Memos can't be picked directly from a web page, so the Save to Files step matters.
              5. 
  
            
  
            On Android: the phone's own recorder
            
  
              
    
            1. Find the app: Recorder on a Google Pixel, Voice Recorder on a Samsung Galaxy. Other brands ship their own; any app that records sound only will do. Don't use the camera.
              2. 
    
            2. Record: tap Record, then Stop. Samsung asks for a name before you tap Save.
              3. 
    
            3. Name it the same way: Q03 Maria, and Q00 for the slate.
              4. 
    
            4. To upload: touch and hold the recording and tap Share (on a Pixel, Share > File > Audio). Save it to the phone's files or to Drive, then open the upload link in Chrome and add the recordings from there.
              5. 
  
            
  
            Menus move between versions and brands. Have one person on each kind of phone walk through this once before the session.
            

            

            
  
            Uploading your recordings
            
  
            For Role Call, every recording goes to one MASV upload portal. Send all of your answers at once, in one upload; there is no field per question, and the files are sorted afterward by their names.
            
  
  
            rolecall.portal.massive.io
            
  
            The portal closes at midnight at the end of Tuesday, November 10, 2026 (Eastern time). Upload before then; after that the link stops taking files.
            
  
              
    
            1. Name every file first: question number and speaker, like Q03 Maria, with Q00 for the slate (see Recording with what you have).
              2. 
    
            2. Save them to Files (iPhone) or the phone's files or Drive (Android), as above.
              3. 
    
            3. Point the phone camera at the code, or open the link in Safari or Chrome.
              4. 
    
            4. Fill in what the portal asks for (your email, and your name in the message), add all your recordings and send. Keep the page open until it says the upload is done.
              5. 
    
            5. Keep your own copies until you hear the season is assembled.
              6. 
  
            
  
            Download the upload QR code to print on a handout or a slide. Keep a white margin around it, and print it at least 2 cm (about an inch) across.
            

            

            
  
            Consent and care
            
  
              
    
            1. Ask on the record. Each person says their name and a clear yes to taking part, and to how the recording will be used and where it will be published.
              2. 
    
            2. Say what's in and what's out. Full name or first name, public or private feed. Let people choose.
              3. 
    
            3. Let people pull an answer. Give a contact and a date by which they can ask for something to be cut, and honor it.
              4. 
    
            4. Protect third parties. Stories mention students, colleagues and family. Cut names and identifying details of people who didn't agree to be in it.
              5. 
    
            5. Minors need more. If students are recorded, follow your institution's media-release policy and get guardian consent first.
              6. 
    
            6. Keep the raw files safe and decide in advance who can access them and for how long.
              7. 
  
            
  
            This is practice, not legal advice. Institutions often have their own release forms; use them.
            

            

            
  
            Cleaning the sound
            
  
            Fix in this order, and do as little as possible at each step. Heavy processing makes voices sound underwater.
            
  
              
    
            1. Trim the dead air at the start and end.
              2. 
    
            2. Reduce noise and echo. AI enhancers can rescue a rough phone recording, and can also change how a voice sounds. Compare before and after; if it no longer sounds like the person, back off.
              3. 
    
            3. Even out the level with gentle compression so quiet and loud moments sit closer together.
              4. 
    
            4. Match loudness across every clip. Apple's published target is about −16 LKFS (±1 dB) with true peaks no higher than −1 dBFS. Hitting one target across all clips is what makes a collage of many phones sound like one show.
              5. 
  
            
  
            Export a lossless master (WAV) to keep, and an MP3 or AAC for publishing. For speech, mono at 64–128 kbps is within Apple's recommended range.
            

            

            
  
            Editing for content
            
  
              
    
            1. Listen to everything once before cutting anything. Mark the moments that made you stop.
              2. 
    
            2. Order by contrast, not by speaker. Put a funny answer after a heavy one; put the newest teacher after the longest-serving.
              3. 
    
            3. Cut to the story, keep the breath. Remove false starts and repetition; leave pauses and laughter. Removing every "um" makes people sound scripted.
              4. 
    
            4. Let the question frame each episode. Read it once at the top, in the facilitator's voice or a rotating one. Then let the answers carry it.
              5. 
    
            5. Keep episodes short. A collage holds attention for less time than a conversation. Cut until you miss something, then put one thing back.
              6. 
    
            6. Make a transcript. It makes the episodes accessible, searchable and quotable.
              7. 
  
            

            

            
  
            Publishing
            
  
            A podcast is an RSS feed. A host stores the audio and publishes the feed; directories like Apple Podcasts, Spotify and YouTube read it.
            
  
              
    
            1. Pick a host. Free hosts exist; read what they take in return (ad revenue share, limits, export). Make sure you can move your feed later.
              2. 
    
            2. Make square cover art. Apple asks for 1400 to 3000 pixels on a side. It has to read at thumbnail size: few words, big.
              3. 
    
            3. Submit the feed once to each directory. Apple through Apple Podcasts Connect. YouTube can ingest a podcast RSS feed and makes a still-image video from your show art for each episode.
              4. 
    
            4. Launch with several episodes, not one. A season made in a day can go up all at once, or weekly to keep people coming back.
              5. 
    
            5. Give it back to the room. The first audience is the people who made it. Send them the link before anyone else.
              6.

            Common mistakes
            
  
            The advice podcasters repeat most to beginners, from guides and forums.
            
  
              
    
            1. Buying gear first. A quiet, soft room and a steady phone will carry you a long way. Upgrade once you know what's actually wrong with your sound.
              2. 
    
            2. Not checking the recording. The most common loss is an answer recorded to a muted or wrong input. Test, every time.
              3. 
    
            3. Over-editing. Polished to nothing. Edit for content first; sound comes second.
              4. 
    
            4. Launching one episode and stalling. Have several done before anyone hears the first.
              5. 
    
            5. No reason to listen. Know who it's for and why they'd press play. A community podcast's first answer is: the people in it, and the people like them.
              6. 
    
            6. Underestimating the hours. Each finished minute takes many minutes of listening and editing. Plan time, not just recording.
              7. 
  
            

            

            
  
            Checklist
            
  
              
    
            1. Questions written, read aloud and checked for hidden assumptions.
              2. 
    
            2. A place set up to collect answers, with one slot per question.
              3. 
    
            3. Consent wording ready; release forms if your institution needs them.
              4. 
    
            4. Your own opening story, three to four minutes.
              5. 
    
            5. Pairs formed; quiet corners mapped.
              6. 
    
            6. Phones charged, on Do Not Disturb, test recording played back.
              7. 
    
            7. Hour two recorded.
              8. 
    
            8. Clips cleaned and matched to one loudness target.
              9. 
    
            9. Episodes cut, transcripts made, cover art tested small.
              10. 
    
            10. Feed published; link sent to everyone who took part.
              11. 
  
            

            

            
  
            Sources and foundations
            
  
            The method comes from paired-interview exercises run with the fellows of an art and design incubator, and from oral history and community radio practice: people interviewing each other, the collage as the form. The question sequence was written for Art Day at FIU, for Miami-Dade County Public Schools teachers.
