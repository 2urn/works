G-code is the path a machine follows to cut, carve or plot. Here every cut becomes a current, and thousands of particles follow it. Load your own G-code, type a word, or trace a picture or the camera. Many buttons do more when you hold them (a long press; a right-click on a computer). Each one is marked Hold below.

What plays, how it moves the field, and a short lesson for each piece of music: tap a lesson to open it, play to hear it, and try to hear a variation (it goes into your Bytebeat box).

  1. Whatever plays is listened to and split into five signals: bass (30 to 150 Hz), mid (150 to 2,000 Hz), high (2,000 to 12,000 Hz), level (all three together) and beat (a sudden jump in the bass). Each rests at zero and rises with the sound.

    A look that has no sound links of its own still answers: louder makes the particles faster, and each beat makes the field stronger for a moment. Looks with their own links (the Beat and Byte looks) say exactly what moves what.

  2. Five drum patterns, synthesized here (no recordings): a kick, a snare, a closed hat and a bass note on a grid of sixteen steps to the bar.

    four
    Four on the floor, 120 bpm: a kick on every beat, the snare on two and four, hats between. The steadiest pulse; best for beat.
    broken
    Broken 96: the kick lands off the beat, so the field lurches instead of pumping.
    half
    Halftime 70: the snare only on three. Slow, heavy, lots of space.
    fibo
    Fibonacci 110: kicks on steps 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 and 13, so the gaps grow 1, 1, 2, 3, 5. Hats on every step, so high never rests.
    pulse
    Pulse 60: one kick a bar and one hat. Almost silence; each event reads clearly in the field.

  3. A bytebeat is a whole piece of music written as one line of arithmetic. A counter, t, goes up 8,000 times a second. The formula turns each value of t into a number, and the last eight bits of that number (0 to 255) are the height of the sound wave at that instant. Nothing is recorded and nothing is sequenced: rhythm, melody and texture all fall out of the arithmetic.

    Three moves do most of the work. Shifting slows a counter down: t>>10 goes up by one every 1,024 steps, about every 0.13 seconds, so it counts musical steps. Multiplying raises the pitch: t*m is a buzzy sawtooth at 31.25 × m Hz. Masking with & keeps only some bits, which switches notes on and off.

  4. t*(42&t>>10) The step counter t>>10 is masked by 42 (binary 101010), so the multiplier walks through 0, 2, 8, 10, 32, 34, 40 and 42: silences, low notes and high notes, in a fixed order that repeats every 64 steps (about 8 seconds). That is why it sounds composed. Called the 42 melody; at least three people found it on their own.

    t*((t>>12|t>>8)&(n*7)&t>>4) A slow counter (every half second) and a faster one (every 32 ms) are combined with | to make the steps of the ladder. Masking with t>>4, which flickers every 2 ms, makes each note buzz like a fast arpeggio. n decides which bits may sound: n 9 gives 63, every note; n 1 gives 7, only the low ones.

  5. t&t>>8 The slow part of the counter masks the fast part, so bits of a ramp get through only when the slow bits allow. The pattern repeats inside itself at every scale; drawn as a picture it is a Sierpiński triangle, which is why these are called Sierpiński harmonies. One cycle lasts about 8 seconds.

    t&t>>((l>>1)+5)|t*2&t>>(o+5) Two fractal layers, the second an octave up (t*2), laid over each other with |. l sets how slowly the first layer unfolds and o the second; when they differ, the two patterns drift against each other like threads in a weave.

  6. t*2&t>>9|t*3&t>>8|t*(p+2)&t>>10 Three voices at pitches in the ratio 2 : 3 : p+2. With p at 2 that is 2 : 3 : 4, an octave and a fifth: open, like a power chord. Each voice is gated by a counter of a different speed, so they enter on different rhythms and the chord keeps re-voicing itself. Move p: 3 gives 5 (a major third), 4 gives 6, 5 gives 7 (the bluesy seventh of the harmonic series).

  7. t*(((t>>9)^((t>>9)-1)^1)%(n+3)) Here the step counter (every 64 ms) is compared with the step before it. That comparison is small on most steps and grows on every second, fourth, eighth and sixteenth step, like the marks on a ruler, so accents arrive in waves of doubling size: a tide. % folds the result into n+3 values, so n picks which notes the waves land on. Every other step is silent, which gives it its pulse.

  8. In the drawer, under Bytebeat, the letters n, l, o, p and the rate are sliders. Change one and the playing song changes at once. Each has its ~: give it a shape (sine, triangle, random steps…) and it moves by itself; or give it x or y and your finger plays it.

    x
    Where your finger is across the screen: nothing at the left edge, all the way at the right.
    y
    How high your finger is: nothing at the bottom, all the way at the top.
    touch
    How fast your finger moves.

    The four Byte looks are played by your fingers already: drag across the screen to move the letters and up for the rate; lift and the song settles back. (Fingers paint direction instead while steer is on.)

  9. Hold the speaker and tap write (or open the drawer to Bytebeat) and type in the box; Ctrl/⌘+Enter or Play formula. While yours plays, playing again swaps it without stopping the clock, so you can change it as it runs. Yours is kept on this device.

    t
    The counter, 8,000 a second.
    >> k
    Slower by half, k times over. >>10 is about a step; >>13 about a second.
    * m
    Higher by m.
    &
    Keep only the shared bits: gates, rhythm.
    |
    Either: layers.
    ^
    One or the other: odd, glassy mixes.
    % k
    Fold into k values: a scale, a loop.
    n l o p
    The sliders. a b c are 4.

  10. Bytebeat was set off in September 2011 by viznut (Ville-Matias Heikkilä), with a video of seven one-line C programs, and grew in the communities that took them up. He wrote it up that December in Discovering novel computer music techniques by exploring the space of short computer programs, which also tells how the 42 melody was found separately by at least three people, and why t&t>>8 and its relatives are called Sierpiński harmonies.

    The engine here, and the ladder, weave, chord and tide formulas, come from Bytebeat Reef, a sister instrument in this studio. The five beats are written for Current.
